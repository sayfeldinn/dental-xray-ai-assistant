| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| 1 | How is this different from Diagnocat or Pearl? | Those products focus on detecting findings on radiographs, and they do it well. DentalAI's contribution is the loop around detection: the dentist must confirm each finding, confirmed findings drive a treatment plan with drug-safety checks, everything lands in the patient's record, and corrections are logged for retraining. We claim the workflow, not a better detector. | 9 · Competitive landscape |
| 2 | What is your test-set performance, and on which data? | On a held-out benchmark of 23 panoramic images with 122 detections, the shipped pipeline gave the correct finding label 97.54% of the time, the correct tooth 76.23%, and both 75.41%. That is a small set from public DENTEX-style data, so we present it as an early result, not clinical performance. | 10 · Data & training; 5 · Results |
| 3 | Your notebook shows mAP50 = 0.922. Why isn't that on the results slide? | Those runs validated on their own training folder (val = train), so they measure fit, not generalization, and that detector is not the shipped one. We only report the held-out benchmark as performance. | 10 · Data & training |
| 4 | Who is liable for a wrong dose? | The treating dentist, exactly as today: the system never finalises a prescription. Every drug is a proposal; a warning needs explicit acknowledgement and a contraindicated drug needs a written reason, both stored with the dentist's id for audit. | 14 · Clinical rules |
| 5 | Does the model really learn from corrections? | Not yet. Every rejection, relabel, missed lesion and override is logged in an append-only table and queued for the ML service, but no retraining run has been done. Retraining from those logs is a stated next step. | 11 · Limitations |
| 6 | What happens when a tooth is missing? | Our earlier per-quadrant enumerators counted teeth in sequence, so a missing tooth shifted the numbering. The shipped version replaced them with a Random Forest that uses spatial features, but tooth numbering is still our weakest output at 76.23%, so the dentist confirms every tooth number. | 11 · Limitations |
| 7 | How do you handle patient privacy? | We have used only synthetic patients and public X-rays. Every request needs a signed-in dentist who sees only their own patients; the database denies all direct client access, and images sit in a private bucket behind one-hour signed links. Compliance with Egypt's PDPL 151/2020 is planned before any pilot. | 12 · Privacy & compliance |
| 8 | Are the drug rules clinically validated? | They are source-backed — every dose and rule cites ADA 2019, AHA 2021, AAPD or AGS Beers 2023 — but no clinician has reviewed them yet, and the system says "pending validation" everywhere. Clinician review is the first step of the pilot. | 14 · Clinical rules |
| 9 | Have you tested it in a real clinic? | Not yet; there is no pilot recorded. The full loop has been run end to end on a live database with synthetic patients and public X-rays, and the next step is a supervised pilot with our partner dentist. | 11 · Limitations; 7 · Next steps |
| 10 | How do you know the software works? | 171 backend and 14 frontend automated tests run in CI on every push, including the three demo branches: no drug, drug without conflict, and drug with conflict. Security tests check that sign-in is required and that one dentist cannot reach another's patients. | 13 · Testing & QA |
| 11 | Will it work on our clinic's X-ray machine? | We expect a domain gap: the model was trained and tested only on public data. That is why the dentist reviews everything, and why the pilot includes measuring performance on local images and retraining if needed. | 11 · Limitations |
| 12 | Why does the AI propose antibiotics at all? | Only when the dentist reports an active infection; plain caries never gets antibiotics, which follows ADA antibiotic-stewardship guidance. A penicillin allergy automatically switches the proposal to clindamycin with a visible warning. | 14 · Clinical rules; 6 · Safety by design |
